Aliyasantana

A distinctive matrilineal inheritance system: property passed through the female line, nephews inherited rather than sons, and women held real importance in family structure.

  • Matrilineal
  • Joint family (kutumba)
  • Once codified in law

Long before "matrilineal" was a term anthropologists reached for, several communities across Tulu Nadu — most notably the Bunts, alongside some other local communities — organized family life around it. Aliyasantana is a distinctive system of matrilineal inheritance and family structure: property and lineage passed through the female line, and it shaped everyday family life for generations.

How it worked

Under Aliyasantana, property belonged to the family as a whole — the kutumba — rather than to individual men, and lineage was traced through mothers rather than fathers. Strikingly, when it came to inheriting headship and managing family property, it was a man's sister's sons (his aliya, or nephews) who inherited — not his own sons, who instead belonged to their mother's family line. Joint families lived together in an ancestral house, often called a guthu or bari, under the stewardship of a senior member of the matrilineal line.

Security for womenBecause property and belonging ran through the female line, women held a structurally secure place within their natal family for life — a markedly different arrangement from the patrilineal norms found in much of the rest of India.

Not unique, but distinctive

Aliyasantana has a well-known parallel just south along the coast: the Marumakkattayam system historically practiced by Kerala's Nair community and others. Both systems reflect a broader pattern of matrilineal social organization found in pockets along India's southwestern coast — though Aliyasantana developed its own specific customs, terminology, and legal codification within Tulu Nadu.

Once formally recognized

Aliyasantana customs were codified and given legal standing during the colonial and early post-independence period, governing inheritance for the communities that followed it.

A 20th-century decline

Broader Hindu personal law reforms and the shift toward smaller, nuclear households gradually eroded the system's everyday practice over the last century.

A living legacy

Few families now follow Aliyasantana rules to the letter, but its legacy hasn't disappeared — ancestral guthu houses, matrilineal surnames and family identities, and a cultural memory of women's standing within the family all still echo through Tulu Nadu communities that once organized their lives around it.
