Where it comes from

The language and the land

Tulu's story and Tulu Nadu's story are the same river, told from two banks.

History of the Tulu language

Tulu belongs to the South Dravidian language family, related to Kannada, Malayalam, and Tamil, but standing apart with its own grammar, vocabulary, and, historically, its own script.

  • ◈

    Ancient roots

    Linguists trace Tulu's separation from related Dravidian tongues back well over a thousand years, shaped in relative isolation by the Western Ghats and the Arabian Sea.

  • ◈

    The Tigalari script

    For centuries Tulu was written in Tigalari (also called the Tulu script), a script closely related to Malayalam's, once used across the region for religious and literary Sanskrit manuscripts as well as Tulu itself.

  • ◈

    Tulu Mahabharato

    Composed by the poet Arunabja around the 15th century, this retelling of the Mahabharata is the oldest substantial work of Tulu literature known to survive.

  • ◈

    An oral epic tradition

    Long before — and alongside — written literature, Tulu carried the Paddanas: sung oral epics performed during Bhuta Kola spirit-worship rituals, passed down through generations of hereditary singers.

  • ◈

    Kannada script era

    As printing spread and Kannada became the region's administrative language, Tulu came to be written mainly in the Kannada script — the convention still most widely used today.

  • ◈

    A living revival

    Today, universities, the Tulu Sahitya Academy, and community groups document Tulu, teach the Tigalari script, and campaign for the language's formal recognition.

History of Tulu Nadu, the region

Tulu Nadu — "the land of the Tulu people" — runs along the coast from the Chandragiri river in the south to the Kali river in the north, roughly today's Dakshina Kannada and Udupi districts of Karnataka and Kasaragod district of Kerala.

  • ◈

    The Alupa dynasty

    For over a millennium, the Alupas ruled the coast from their capital near present-day Mangalore, leaving behind temples, inscriptions, and a distinct regional identity.

  • ◈

    A crossroads of trade

    Coastal ports connected Tulu Nadu to Arab, Persian, and later European traders, bringing spices, textiles, and ideas through harbours that still anchor the region's towns.

  • ◈

    Vijayanagara & the Nayakas

    The region later came under the Vijayanagara Empire and the Keladi Nayakas, who left their mark on temple architecture and local governance across the coast.

  • ◈

    Colonial rule

    Portuguese, then British administration reorganised the coast into the South Canara district, a boundary whose legacy still roughly shapes the Tulu-speaking region.

  • ◈

    Kambala & Yakshagana

    Buffalo-racing Kambala festivals and night-long Yakshagana theatre — dance, music, and myth performed in elaborate costume — remain the region's most visible living traditions.

  • ◈

    Tulu Nadu today

    Split across two modern states, the region is united by cuisine, festival, and language rather than a single administrative line — and by an active movement for Tulu Nadu statehood.
