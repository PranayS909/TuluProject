Kannada Script Today

As printing spread, Tulu came to be written mainly in the Kannada script — the convention most widely used for the language today.

  • Newspapers & books
  • Taught in schools
  • Print-era shift

Open a Tulu newspaper column, a modern poetry collection, or a Tulu-language signboard on the coast today, and you'll almost always see it written in the Kannada script — not Tigalari, Tulu's own historical script. This is the practical, everyday convention most Tulu speakers and writers use now.

Why Kannada, and not Tigalari

The shift wasn't a single decision but a gradual byproduct of geography and schooling. Tulu Nadu sits within Karnataka (with a slice in Kerala), and Kannada is the state's official language and the medium most Tulu speakers are formally taught to read and write in school. As mass education and printing presses spread through the 19th and 20th centuries, it was simply more practical to write an unwritten-at-scale language using a script everyone already knew, rather than teach Tigalari — a specialist script by that point — to a whole new readership.

A reasonably close fitKannada and Tulu share much of their consonant and vowel inventory, both being South Dravidian languages, which made adapting Kannada letters to spell Tulu words relatively straightforward — even if a few Tulu sounds don't map perfectly onto Kannada letters.

What gets written in Kannada script

Print & media

Tulu newspapers, magazines, and community bulletins are typeset in Kannada script for a readership already literate in it.

Modern literature

Contemporary Tulu poetry, plays, and prose — including work published by the Tulu Sahitya Academy — are printed in Kannada script.

Digital & social media

Tulu content online, from social posts to song lyrics, is typed almost entirely using Kannada-script keyboards.

Film & subtitles

The growing Tulu cinema industry uses Kannada script for titles, credits, and promotional text.

Convenience, and a trade-off

Writing Tulu in Kannada script keeps the language visible, printable, and easy to learn for anyone already schooled in Kannada — a real practical win for a language without official state status of its own. But it also means most Tulu speakers today have no everyday exposure to the script their language was historically recorded in. That gap is exactly what the Tigalari revival movement is working to close, teaching the historical script alongside — not instead of — the Kannada-script convention most people use day to day.
