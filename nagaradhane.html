Nagaradhane

Serpent worship honouring deities believed to protect the land — central to Nagapanchami, when communities gather in temples for prayers and rituals.

  • Serpent deities
  • Sacred groves
  • Nagapanchami

Alongside Bhoota Kola, Nagaradhane — serpent worship — is one of Tulu Nadu's most widespread devotional traditions. Serpents, seen as powerful guardian deities connected to fertility, family lineage, and the land itself, are honoured in household shrines, sacred groves, and dedicated temples across the region.

Naga stones and sacred groves

Worship is often centered on naga kallu (naga stones) — carved images of serpent deities installed beneath trees or within small groves known as naga bana, frequently found on the grounds of ancestral family properties. These sites are treated as inviolable and are maintained with regular offerings, oil lamps, and periodic poojas performed by family priests.

Guardians of the landNaga deities are widely believed to protect the family and its property, and to influence fertility and prosperity — which is why maintaining their shrine is treated as a serious, ongoing family responsibility, not a one-time ritual.

Nagamandala — the elaborate ceremony

The most striking expression of Nagaradhane is Nagamandala, an elaborate, multi-day ceremony centered on an intricate mandala — a large, colorful serpent motif drawn on the ground using natural powders. Ritual specialists prepare the mandala with great care, and a performer, again in a state of possession, is believed to embody the serpent deity and moves across the design through the night as part of the ceremony.

Nagapanchami

An annual festival day dedicated to serpent worship, when households and temples across the region hold special prayers and rituals honouring naga deities.

Family-specific rites

Some families observe additional serpent-related rituals believed to address specific astrological or ancestral concerns, guided by family priests.

Part of a wider ritual landscape

Naga worship doesn't stand apart from Tulu Nadu's other ritual traditions — sacred groves and naga shrines are frequently found alongside or near the sthana sites used for Bhoota Kola, reflecting a broader coastal Karnataka worldview in which land, ancestry, and the divine are closely intertwined.

Sources consulted: News Karnataka — Nagaradhane: The sacred serpent worship of Tulunadu, Wikipedia — Nagaradhane. Ritual details and the specific communities who perform Nagamandala can vary by locality — treat this as a general introduction.
