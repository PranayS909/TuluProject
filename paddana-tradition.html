The Paddana Tradition

Long before — and alongside — written literature, Tulu carried sung oral epics called Paddanas, performed during Bhoota Kola rituals by hereditary singers.

  • Sung, not written
  • Tied to Bhoota Kola
  • Hereditary performers

While the Tulu Mahabharato represents Tulu's written literary tradition, most of Tulu Nadu's oldest stories were never written down at all — they were sung. These sung narrative poems are called Paddanas, and they form one of the richest oral epic traditions in South India.

Performed, not just recited

Paddanas are most closely associated with Bhoota Kola, the spirit-possession ritual in which a performer channels a local deity or ancestral spirit (a bhoota or daiva). During these all-night ceremonies, ritual singers chant the Paddana connected to that particular spirit — recounting its origin story, its deeds, and its claim to be honored by the community. The song is treated as part of the ritual itself, not mere entertainment alongside it.

Carried by specific familiesPaddana performance has traditionally been the responsibility of particular hereditary communities of ritual specialists, who learn and transmit the songs across generations largely by ear rather than from a written text.

What Paddanas preserve

Because they long predate widespread literacy in the region, Paddanas function as a kind of living archive — carrying local genealogies, the origin myths of particular deities and landholding families, moral codes, and details of everyday coastal life from generations past. For linguists, they're also a valuable record of spoken Tulu, since sung oral tradition changes on a different timeline than written literary language.

Night-long performance

Many Paddanas are sung during overnight Bhoota Kola ceremonies, often building toward a climactic sequence as the ritual itself intensifies.

Increasingly transcribed

Folklorists and Tulu scholars have worked to record and publish written transcriptions and translations, since the oral chain of transmission is fragile.

Still sung today

Bhoota Kola remains an active, living ritual practice across Tulu Nadu, which means Paddanas are still being performed, not just archived — a rare case of an oral epic tradition surviving into the present largely intact. That continuity is itself part of the wider story of Tulu's active revival, where documenting living oral tradition sits alongside reviving written scripts.

Sources consulted: general references on Bhoota Kola and Tulu oral tradition, including coverage from The South First and general Tulu-language references. Community and family-specific performance customs vary by village and are best learned from Tulu Nadu practitioners directly.
