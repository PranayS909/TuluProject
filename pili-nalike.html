Pili Nalike

In Tulu, pili means tiger and nalike means dance. Painted from head to toe in stripes, troupes of "tigers" take over the streets during festival season, prowling and leaping to the beat of thundering drums.

If you hear a fast, booming rhythm in the streets of Mangaluru or Udupi during festival season, the tigers are probably out. Pili Nalike (known in Kannada as Huli Vesha) is a folk dance in which performers paint their bodies as tigers, sometimes leopards or cheetahs too, and dance through the town in groups, stopping at homes, shops and street corners.

🪔Why tigers, and when

The tiger is the mount of the goddess Durga, which is why the dance is most closely tied to Dasara (Navaratri), when troupes perform as an offering. It's also a highlight of Krishna Janmashtami (Ashtami) celebrations, especially in Udupi. Many performers take the vesha (costume) as a vow, and the troupe collects offerings as it moves from place to place.

🎨

Hours in the paint chairThe stripes are painted straight onto the skin, a job that can take many hours. Performers often keep the paint on for the whole festival run, dancing for days at a time.

🥁The sound and the moves

🥁 Taase

The dance is driven by taase drums, played in a fast, insistent rhythm that you can hear from streets away. The tigers' steps follow the drummers.

🤸 Show-stopping stunts

Troupes show off acrobatics. A famous one is bending all the way back to pick something up off the ground with the mouth, cheered on by the crowd.

🏆From street custom to big stage

What began as neighbourhood troupes has grown into a major attraction, with organised groups, competitions and crowds that come just to watch the tigers. Through all of it, the dance has stayed rooted in devotion and in the drumming that defines a Tulu Nadu festival.

Sources consulted: Wikipedia: Pili Vesha. Customs differ between troupes and towns. This page is a general orientation, not a substitute for local knowledge.
