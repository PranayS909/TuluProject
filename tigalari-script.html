The Tigalari Script

For centuries, Tulu had a writing system all its own — a script closely related to Malayalam's, once carried by coastal scribes for both Sanskrit manuscripts and Tulu literature.

  • Also called Tulu-Tigalari
  • Grantha-derived
  • Encoded in Unicode 16.0

Long before the Kannada script became the everyday convention for writing Tulu, the language had its own literary hand: Tigalari, sometimes called the Tulu script or Tulu-Tigalari. It belongs to the same family of scripts as Malayalam — both descend from the medieval Grantha script, which itself traces back to the Brahmi writing system used across ancient India.

ಗ್ರಂಥA shared ancestor with Malayalam

Tigalari and Malayalam scripts diverged from the same Grantha lineage, which explains why a reader familiar with old Malayalam letterforms can often recognize the family resemblance in Tigalari manuscripts — rounded, looping strokes suited to writing on palm leaf with an iron stylus. Despite the resemblance, Tigalari developed its own conventions and was never simply "Malayalam used for Tulu" — it grew into a distinct script tradition of the southwestern coast.

Not just for TuluTigalari's biggest historical role was actually recording Sanskrit — Vedic texts, commentaries, and Brahminical literature copied by scholarly communities across Tulu Nadu. Its use for Tulu-language literature, like the Tulu Mahabharato, sat alongside this broader Sanskrit manuscript tradition.

From palm leaf to decline

Tigalari was traditionally inscribed on palm-leaf manuscripts (grantha), bundled and preserved by families and monasteries along the coast. As printing presses spread through the 19th and 20th centuries and Kannada became the language of schooling and administration in the region, Tigalari gradually fell out of everyday use. Fewer people learned to read it, and Tulu writing shifted almost entirely to the more widely taught Kannada script — the shift explored in more detail on the Kannada script today page.

A digital second life

Interest in Tigalari never fully disappeared. Scholars, heritage groups, and script enthusiasts have worked for years to document its letterforms and teach it to new generations. That effort reached a major milestone when the Tulu-Tigalari script was formally added to the Unicode Standard (Unicode 16.0), giving it a standardized digital encoding for the first time — a prerequisite for fonts, keyboards, and searchable digitized manuscripts.

Script classes

Community organizations and university programs now run workshops teaching Tigalari letterforms to interested learners and researchers.

Manuscript digitization

Palm-leaf archives are being photographed and cataloged so fragile originals aren't the only copies of centuries-old texts.
