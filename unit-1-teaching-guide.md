# Unit 1: First Words — Teaching Guide

This is the full teaching content behind Unit 1 of the roadmap (Greetings, Numbers 1–20, the Bonus round, and Family Words), pulled together so you can actually run the lessons rather than just show the vocab bubbles on the site. Every phrase below is sourced from a real Tulu-learning site or blog — mostly [Easy Tulu](https://www.easytulu.com/) — and cited at the end of each lesson. Where I've composed an example sentence myself (to give you something to teach *with*, not just a word list), I've flagged it clearly. Tulu has no single standardized spelling, so treat all Roman-script forms here as one common convention, not the only correct one, and get anything you plan to publish checked by a native speaker first.

A general note on pronunciation before you start: Easy Tulu's own guide is the best single reference, and it's worth reading in full or linking students to it directly — [How to Pronounce Tulu (Easy Tulu)](https://www.easytulu.com/p/how-to-pronounce.html).

## Before You Start: Pronunciation Primer

Tulu is written here in Roman script (there's no widely-known native keyboard for the historical Tigalari script), so the first thing to teach — before any vocabulary — is how to read that Roman transliteration.

**Short vowels**

| Letter | Sounds like |
|---|---|
| a | "u" in *but* |
| i | "i" in *pig* |
| u | "u" in *put* |
| e | "e" in *egg* |
| o | "o" in *obey* |
| ae | "a" in *man* |

**Long vowels** (held twice as long as their short counterpart; written with a capital letter in most of the site's vocab lists, e.g. *A*, *I*, *U*)

| Letter | Sounds like |
|---|---|
| A | "a" in *car* |
| I | "ea" in *eat* |
| U | "oo" in *mood* |
| E | "a" in *ate* |
| ai | "i" in *ice* |
| O | "o" in *old* |
| au | "ow" in *how* |

Two things worth calling out explicitly with students on day one:

Every Tulu word that ends in a consonant carries a very short, almost-swallowed "half-u" sound at the end — the same kind of schwa you'd hear in Malayalam. You'll see it marked with an apostrophe when it shows up mid-word (e.g. *batt'ND*). It's why words like *ushArullae* or *namaskAra* have that soft trailing vowel even though nothing is technically "spelled" there.

Tulu also has aspirated consonant pairs — *kh, gh, Ch, jh, Th, Dh, ph, bh* — which are the same base sound as *k, g, ch, j, t, d, p, b* but said with a small extra puff of air. English speakers tend to under-aspirate these; it's worth demonstrating out loud rather than just describing it.

*Source: [Easy Tulu — How to Pronounce](https://www.easytulu.com/p/how-to-pronounce.html)*

## Lesson 1: Greetings

**Objective:** By the end of this lesson, a student can greet someone, ask and answer "how are you," introduce themselves, and say goodbye — in both the formal and informal register.

### Vocabulary

| Tulu | English | Notes |
|---|---|---|
| namaskAra / namastE | hello | Formal, safe default with anyone |
| solmelu | hi / thank you | Sources give both meanings — confirm which sense with a speaker before teaching it as fact |
| svAgata | welcome | |
| Encha ullar? | how are you? | Formal (*-ar* ending marks respect) |
| encha ulla? | how are you? | Informal |
| ushArullae / saukhya | I am fine | |
| bEga ushArAle | get well soon | Formal |
| bEga ushArAla | get well soon | Informal |
| pudar enchine? | what is your name? | Formal |
| pudar enchina? | what is your name? | Informal |
| undu dAde? | what is this? | Formal |
| undu dAda? | what is this? | Informal |
| Ir oltAr? | where are you from? | Formal |
| irena Uru olpa? | where are you from? | Alternate phrasing |
| upakAra AND | thank you (for help) | Distinct from *solmelu* |
| dayamalt / dayadId | please | |
| enk gottuNDu | I know | |
| enk gottuji | I don't know | |
| mallejji / mallaett | no problem | |
| kEnle | listen! | Formal |
| kEn | listen! | Informal |
| barpae | bye | |
| kuDora tikk'ga | see you again | |

### A sample exchange

This isn't taken verbatim from a source — it's built out of the vocabulary above so you have something to model and drill with students. Have a speaker sanity-check the phrasing before you rely on it as "correct Tulu":

> **A:** namaskAra! encha ulla?
> **B:** ushArullae, solmelu. Ir?
> **A:** enk pun ushArullae. pudar enchina?
> **B:** enna pudar Asha. Ir oltAr?
> **A:** barpae, kuDora tikk'ga!

*(Hello! How are you? — I'm fine, thanks. You? — I'm fine too. What's your name? — My name is Asha. Where are you from? — Bye, see you again!)*

### Teaching notes

The formal/informal split shows up consistently as a change in the verb ending — *-ar/-Ar* forms are formal, the shorter vowel-only forms are informal. It's worth teaching this as a pattern from lesson one rather than as isolated vocabulary pairs, since it recurs constantly through the rest of the course. Also flag *solmelu* as a word to double-check — it surfaces in different sources meaning both "hi" and "thank you," and that kind of ambiguity is common with Tulu's informal, largely undocumented vocabulary.

### Suggested activities

Pair students up and have them run the sample exchange above, swapping in their own names. A quick round-robin drill of formal vs. informal "how are you" (call out a social context — talking to a grandparent vs. a friend — and have students pick the right register) reinforces the formality pattern well.

### Sources

[Easy Tulu — Lesson 41: Useful Phrases in Tulu](https://www.easytulu.com/2016/12/tulu-lesson-41-useful-phrases-in-tulu.html) (60+ phrases with Tulu/English/Kannada), [Raveesh Kumar — Learn Tulu Online: Commonly Used Phrases](https://www.raveeshkumar.com/2009/05/learn-tulu-online-commonly-used-phrases.html)

## Lesson 2: Numbers 1–20

**Objective:** Count from 1 to 20, and recognize the pattern that generates the teen numbers.

### Vocabulary

| # | Tulu | # | Tulu |
|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | onji | 11 | pattonji |
| 2 | raDD | 12 | padiraDD |
| 3 | mUji | 13 | padimUji |
| 4 | nAl | 14 | padinAl |
| 5 | ain | 15 | padinain |
| 6 | Aji | 16 | padinAji |
| 7 | El | 17 | padinEl |
| 8 | enma | 18 | padinenma |
| 9 | orumba | 19 | padinorumba |
| 10 | patt | 20 | irva |

### Teaching notes

This is a great lesson for pattern-spotting rather than rote memorization: once a student knows 1–10, the teens (11–19) are just *padi-* (a form of "ten") prefixed to the digit — *padi + raDD = padiraDD* ("twelve"), *padi + nAl = padinAl* ("fourteen"), and so on. Eleven is the odd one out, using *patt-* rather than *padi-* (*pattonji*). Twenty (*irva*) breaks the pattern entirely and is worth teaching as a standalone word, since 21–99 would extend from it rather than from *patt*.

The source lesson also introduces the tag-question particles *ata?* ("isn't it / right?") and *ijja?* ("isn't it? / or not?"), which function like English "...right?" tacked onto a statement. They're not number-specific, but the lesson pairs them with counting examples, so it's a natural place to introduce them — e.g. confirming a quantity: "irva rupAy, ata?" ("twenty rupees, right?").

One honest gap: the source material doesn't give an explicit word for "how much / how many" as a standalone interrogative (the kind you'd need for "how much does this cost?"). If you need that phrase for a shopping/market context, it's worth sourcing separately (the Unit 2 "Food & Market" lesson has a related phrase, *nekk Eth?*, "how much is this?") or confirming with a speaker before teaching it here.

### Suggested activities

Count objects around the room in Tulu. Have students say their age, a phone-number-style digit string, or count off in a line using *onji, raDD, mUji...* Drill the teens specifically by having students build them live from the *padi-* + digit pattern rather than just reading them off the table.

### Sources

[Easy Tulu — Lesson 5: More Interrogative](https://www.easytulu.com/2016/02/tulu-lesson-5-more-interrogative.html)

## Bonus: Sounds & Rhythm

The roadmap lists this stop as "tongue-twisters," but it's worth being upfront with students (and honest with yourself as the teacher): real, documented Tulu tongue-twisters are genuinely thin on the ground online. A search across language blogs, forums, and community sites didn't turn up a verified set worth teaching as authentic folk material — the strongest lead was a forum thread that wasn't accessible to pull from directly. Rather than invent Tulu phrases and pass them off as traditional tongue-twisters, the honest move is to run this stop as a **pronunciation-drill bonus** using the real sounds and vocabulary from Lessons 1 and 2, and treat actual tongue-twister collection as an open task.

### What to actually teach here

Drill the short/long vowel pairs from the Pronunciation Primer back-to-back so students hear the length difference clearly: *a/A, i/I, u/U, e/E, o/O*. Then take words students already know from Lesson 1 and 2 and have them repeat rapid-fire to build fluency with the schwa ending — *ushArullae, namaskAra, padinorumba* are all good candidates, since each has a few syllables and that characteristic trailing half-vowel.

For an actual tongue-twister, the most defensible option is to reach out directly: a Tulu-speaker Facebook or Instagram group, a native-speaker contact, or a classroom guest who grew up speaking Tulu. That will get you something authentic rather than a fabricated one. If it helps as a starting point for that conversation, there's a discussion thread on a Tulu-speakers forum that specifically asked this same question — worth reading even secondhand (via search results) for direction on where to look next.

### Sources

[Quora — What are some Tulu slangs?](https://www.quora.com/What-are-some-tulu-slangs) (starting point, not verified content), Tulu Speakers forum — "Tongue Twisters" thread (found via search, not directly accessible to quote from)

## Lesson 3: Family Words

**Objective:** Name immediate and extended family members, and introduce your own family in simple sentences.

Tulu (like many South Asian languages) has much more specific kinship terminology than English — separate words for elder vs. younger siblings, maternal vs. paternal relatives, and so on. It's worth naming that up front as a feature, not a complication: it reflects how central extended family and relative seniority are in Tulu-speaking coastal Karnataka culture.

### Vocabulary

**Immediate family**

| Tulu | English |
|---|---|
| appae (amma) | mother |
| amme (ayye / poppa) | father |
| mage | son |
| magal | daughter |
| bAlae | child / kid / baby |
| bAlelu / jOkulu | children |

**Siblings**

| Tulu | English |
|---|---|
| palaye (aNNe) | elder brother |
| paldi / pali (akka) | elder sister |
| megye | younger brother |
| megdi / tangaDi | younger sister |

**Grandparents and beyond**

| Tulu | English |
|---|---|
| ajje | grandfather |
| ajji (abba) | grandmother |
| pijje / pijji | great-grandmother |
| pulli | grandchild |
| talli | great-grandchild |

**Spouses and in-laws**

| Tulu | English |
|---|---|
| kaNDane / kaNDani | husband |
| boDedi | wife |
| mAmu | father-in-law (or paternal uncle) |
| mAmi | mother-in-law (or paternal aunt) |
| marmaye | son-in-law |
| marmal | daughter-in-law |

**Extended family**

| Tulu | English |
|---|---|
| tammalae | maternal uncle |
| bhAve | brother-in-law (elder) |
| nanike / maitine | brother-in-law (younger) |
| attai / atyae | sister-in-law |
| maitidi | sister-in-law (younger) |
| arvatte | nephew |

**Other relationship terms**

| Tulu | English |
|---|---|
| kuTuma | family |
| kuTumadaklu | relatives |
| ishTer | friends / well-wishers |
| dOsti | friend / friendship |

### A note on introducing family

To say "this is my [relative]," combine *enna* ("my") with the kinship word — e.g. *enna appae* ("my mother"), *enna palaye* ("my elder brother"). This isn't pulled from a dedicated grammar source, so treat it as a reasonable extrapolation from the *enna pudar* ("my name") pattern in Lesson 1 rather than a confirmed rule, and check it with a speaker before teaching it as gospel.

### A double-check worth flagging to students

*Appae* means "mother" and *amme* means "father" in Tulu — the reverse of Kannada, where the equivalent-sounding words map the other way. It's a genuinely easy mix-up for anyone who already knows some Kannada, so it's worth calling out explicitly rather than letting students absorb it quietly and get it backwards.

### Suggested activities

Have students draw a simple family tree and label each person with the Tulu term, using *enna* + kinship word. Practice distinguishing elder vs. younger sibling terms specifically, since that four-way split (elder brother / younger brother / elder sister / younger sister) doesn't exist in English and needs deliberate drilling.

[Easy Tulu — Family Relationships in Tulu](https://www.easytulu.com/p/family-relationships-in-tulu.html), [TuluBuzz — Family Relationship names in Tulu](https://www.tulubuzz.in/2024/03/Family-Relationship-names-in-tulu.html)

## Unit 1 Checkpoint

Before moving on to Unit 2 (Everyday Tulu), a student should be able to:

Greet someone and respond to "how are you" in both formal and informal registers, introduce themselves and ask someone else's name, count from 1 to 20 and explain how the teen numbers are built, and name at least six to eight family members using the correct Tulu term — including getting the *appae*/*amme* mother/father swap right.

A simple checkpoint quiz could mix all three lessons: give a social context (child greeting a grandparent, two friends meeting) and have the student produce the right greeting register; read out a number 1–20 and have them write or say the Tulu word; and show a simple family tree diagram and have them label three or four relationships correctly.

Total vocabulary introduced in this unit: roughly 20 greeting phrases, 20 numbers, and 25 family/kinship terms — about 65 items across the four stops.

## A note on quality control

Everything above traces back to a real source, cited at the end of each lesson, but almost all of those sources are enthusiast blogs rather than linguistic references, and Tulu doesn't have one standardized Roman spelling — you'll see the same word transliterated slightly differently from site to site. Before this becomes a lesson you actually teach from, it's worth two things: cross-checking anything flagged above with at least one more source, and getting a native Tulu speaker to sanity-check pronunciation and phrasing, especially for the composed example sentences and the *enna* + kinship-word pattern in Lesson 3, neither of which came directly from a source.
